Overview

I built Précis to read thousands of research papers in parallel with LLM agents and turn them into verified, structured summaries and a map of the field. A reader agent moves through each paper's sections with tools instead of receiving the whole text. A critic checks every claim of the summary against the paper and has to quote its evidence verbatim, and summaries with unsupported or contradicted claims are rewritten and checked again. The corpus is then clustered into topics, and Précis writes a report and a searchable static site.

The hard parts surround the model. Thousands of agents share one API with per-minute limits on requests, input tokens and output tokens, so every request goes through one gateway that admits it under an adaptive concurrency window and token buckets, retries failures, caches replies and counts the cost. A run can be killed at any point and resumed without paying twice for any answered request, because a reply's call record and its cache entry are written in one SQLite transaction. Every number on this page comes from offline runs against a fake API with simulated limits and faults: they measure the machinery, not the quality of the summaries, and cost nothing.

Crash recovery and the real corpus

A run killed with SIGKILL loses only what was in memory. On restart, stages left running go back to pending, finished stages are skipped, and an interrupted stage replays its finished turns from the response cache. The one request whose reply the crash lost is sent again; no answered request is paid for twice. The ingest side ran against arXiv itself, at its polite pace of one request every three seconds.

Reading strategies

Reading with tools opens about half of each paper and grows with what it reads, not with what exists: on papers over 12,000 tokens it used a quarter fewer tokens than sending the full text (a median of 12,470 against 16,535), and it was the cheapest of the three strategies because half of its tokens are prompt-cache reads. Through the Message Batches API the same work costs 52% less ($7.70 against $16.14 for 200 papers), at the price of minutes per turn instead of seconds.

How it works

Verify with quotes, and check the quotes

The critic gives each claim, the TL;DR and every finding, a verdict: supported, partial, unsupported or contradicted, each with a verbatim quote from the paper. The quote is then looked up in the paper, with whitespace, case, typographic quotes and dashes normalized, and a “supported” verdict whose quote is not there is downgraded to partial. Before that, a summary whose numbers do not appear in the paper is rejected and the reader is told why. Without evidence a verdict is just another generation; with it, “supported” can be checked.

One gateway that agents don't see

The agents only call create. The gateway admits each request under a concurrency window that works like TCP's: it doubles per round trip in slow start, halves on a 429 or 529 once per congestion event, then grows by one. Token buckets mirror the API's per-minute limits, learned from its rate-limit headers, so requests wait on the client instead of collecting 429s, and a 429's retry-after pauses every admission. Slow start was added after the throughput benchmark showed the additive ramp to be the bottleneck: at a ceiling of 128 it lifted throughput from 130.6 to 165.1 papers per minute.

Never pay twice, even under SIGKILL

Every stage result is keyed by a hash of what determines it (model, prompts, tools, options and inputs), and every reply is cached under a hash of its request, so a rerun skips finished work and an interrupted stage replays its turns for free. The reply's call record and its cache entry used to be two writes; a SIGKILL between them left a paid call with no cached reply, which the next run bought again, about one run in ten. They are now one SQLite transaction. The benchmarks run on a virtual clock that advances only when every task is waiting, so a 2,000-paper run takes about 30 seconds and gives the same result for the same seeds.

Limits

  • Summary quality is not measured yet. That takes a paid run against the API. The evaluation commands (key-point recall without the abstract, claim faithfulness with a quote audit, the strategy comparison) are built and tested offline, and a run can be capped with --max-cost.
  • Offline numbers measure machinery, not judgement. How much a real model chooses to read, and how often it is rejected or revised, will differ.
  • Some PDFs lose structure. About 8% of papers have no usable HTML, and their PDFs, many made with Word, go through a heuristic outline finder; scanned or badly encoded PDFs fail the quality gate by design.
  • Quote checks have gaps. A quote is accepted if it appears anywhere in the paper, not necessarily in the cited section, and the number check cannot see numbers written as words.
  • One machine. One event loop and one SQLite file: plenty for tens of thousands of papers, since the API's rate limit is the bottleneck, but not a distributed system.
