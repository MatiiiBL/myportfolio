Overview

I wrote Apex from scratch in C++20: the software of an autonomous Formula Student Driverless race car, and the simulator it races in. Every run on this page happens in that simulator. The car is staged on a track it has never seen, marked only by blue cones on the left and yellow cones on the right. On lap 1 it finds the cones with a LiDAR and a camera, builds a map while localizing in it (EKF-SLAM: an extended Kalman filter over the car's pose and every cone), and drives a path planned through the cones it has seen so far. Back at the line it closes the map, computes a minimum-curvature raceline and races nine more laps with model predictive control (MPC).

Each part is a known algorithm. The hard part is making them work together on noisy data, inside a real-time budget, and finding out where each one fails. Every algorithm is implemented in the repository, down to the Delaunay triangulation and both quadratic-programming (QP) solvers, and every fast path is checked against a slow, obviously correct one. On eleven simulated tracks it had never seen, three seeds each, the car finished all 33 ten-lap runs without knocking a cone.

How it works

The car never links the simulator

Perception, estimation, planning and control link only the core library; CMake enforces it. Ground truth reaches the car only through truth_id fields used for scoring, and a test proves no decision reads them: relabeling every truth id gives bit-identical commands. Runs are deterministic, one seeded random stream per consumer and one thread per run, so any failure is reproduced with one line of JSON.

Every fast path has a slow oracle

The LiDAR ray caster casts 57,664 rays per scan and is bit-identical to a brute-force every-ray-every-cone reference, 42 times slower. Both QP solvers are checked against OSQP, the vehicle model against analytic cornering limits, the online SLAM map against batch least squares over the whole run, and the Delaunay triangulation against the empty-circumcircle property.

Grip is per axle, not per car

The first speed profile used one friction ellipse for the whole car, and the car slid wide at every corner entry. Under hard braking the load moves to the front while the brakes keep a 60/40 split: at −18.5 m/s² the rear carries 32% of the load and 40% of the braking. The planner now checks each axle's ellipse, and the MPC bounds its acceleration by what each axle has left after the corner's lateral force.

Limits

  • The world is simulated, and simple. The ground is flat, LiDAR scans are instantaneous, knocked cones stay put, sensor noise is Gaussian, and the tires have no temperature or wear. The errors measured here are those of this world, not of a real track.
  • The grip is assumed known. The planner races at 70% of the tires' nominal grip and does not adapt. On a track with 30% less grip than nominal, 6 of 12 runs fail (three leave the track, three stall) and the others knock 20 to 35 cones.
  • 33 runs show typical behavior, not rare failures. About one MPC command in 100,000 reaches the QP solver's iteration limit and falls back to a safe command, so far without a penalty.
  • The localization filter is often overconfident while racing: its pose NEES (normalized estimation error squared) reaches 6 against an ideal of 3, for a reason not yet traced.
  • Compute times come from one machine, an Apple M4, measured with nothing else running.
